# Voice and style guide for professor emails

Professors get dozens of generic, template-like emails every week and delete most of them in seconds. The emails that get answered are short, specific, and clearly written by one particular person who read the professor's work. The goal is not to "disguise" anything. It's to write the email the applicant would write themselves on a good day: in their voice, with real specifics they stand behind.

## 1. Match the applicant's voice
- Read the "Voice samples" in `profile.md` and any edits the user made to earlier drafts. Copy their habits: sentence length, how formal they are, greeting style ("Dear Prof. X" vs "Dear Dr. X"), sign-off, and typical phrases.
- Write the way a strong non-native or native speaker *of their background* actually writes. Slight plainness is fine. Polished marketing prose is not.
- If no samples exist, ask the user for 2–3 emails they've written before you draft.

## 2. Structure (150–220 words, plain text)
1. **Subject:** specific, not salesy. For example: `Prospective PhD student – <topic> (Fall 2027)` or `PhD position <ref no.> – <Name>`.
2. **Opening (1–2 sentences):** who they are, plus why this professor, right away. No warm-up line.
3. **The specific link (2–3 sentences):** one recent paper or project of theirs, what the applicant took from it, and how it connects to something the applicant has actually done. This must be a genuine, checkable detail, not a summary of the abstract.
4. **Evidence (2–3 sentences):** the applicant's most relevant result: a thesis finding, a publication, a skill, with a number if real.
5. **Ask (1–2 sentences):** a clear, small question. "Are you taking PhD students for Fall 2027?" / "Would you be open to a short call?" Mention the funding route if one is relevant (e.g. "I'm also applying for the X scholarship, which requires a supervisor's support").
6. **Sign-off:** name, current role/university, and a link to Google Scholar/GitHub/website if they have one. Mention the attachments in one line ("I've attached my CV and transcripts").

Vary this between professors: change the order of paragraphs 3 and 4, and vary the openings and the asks. No two emails may share more than a sentence or two of boilerplate.

## 3. Phrases and patterns to avoid
These mark an email as generic or machine-written, and get it deleted:
- "I hope this email finds you well", "I am writing to express my keen interest", "I came across your profile"
- "delve", "leverage", "cutting-edge", "groundbreaking", "pivotal", "tapestry", "fostering", "seamlessly", "I am deeply passionate", "resonates with me", "aligns perfectly", "a testament to", "furthermore", "moreover"
- Excessive praise ("your seminal, groundbreaking work")
- Em-dashes (—) used as a stylistic tic. Use commas, full stops, or brackets instead, as most people do in email.
- Triplets of adjectives ("innovative, impactful, and transformative"), and "not only X but also Y"
- Bullet lists or bold text in the body, and headings
- Restating the paper's abstract back to the author
- Perfectly symmetrical paragraphs of equal length; every sentence the same length
- Closing lines like "I look forward to the possibility of contributing to your esteemed lab"
- Curly quotes or odd unicode pasted from documents. Use plain ASCII punctuation.

## 4. Self-check before showing a draft
- [ ] 150–220 words; it reads aloud naturally in the applicant's voice
- [ ] Contains at least one detail that could only be written about *this* professor, and that detail is in "Facts used" with a source
- [ ] Every claim about the applicant is in profile.md or the CV
- [ ] None of the section 3 patterns appear (scan for them explicitly)
- [ ] Name, title, and university spelled correctly; the email address is verified
- [ ] Attachments listed and present on disk
- [ ] Different opening and structure from the previous draft in this batch

## 5. Remind the applicant
The applicant should read every email before it goes out, and be ready to discuss anything in it on a call. If a sentence doesn't sound like them, it gets rewritten.
